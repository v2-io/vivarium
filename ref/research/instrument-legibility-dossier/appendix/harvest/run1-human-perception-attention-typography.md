## Scope — how the question was decomposed

_Decompose along the causal chain a photon takes to become an operator decision — encoding and graphical perception, attention allocation under a competing visual task, low-level legibility/contrast thresholds, and whole-display glanceability models — then add a deliberately adversarial angle that hunts failed replications and boundary conditions so the famous laws are not imported uncritically. Each query is pitched at surveys, standards, and quantitative measurements rather than style advice, since the deliverable needs designable numbers plus explicit epistemic tiering (theoretically principled / empirically established / craft consensus)._

- **broad/foundational — graphical perception and preattentive encoding** — `graphical perception experiments ranking visual encodings Cleveland McGill replication preattentive processing visual pop-out Healey Enns survey Colin Ware perception for design`
  - Establishes the theoretically-principled and empirically-measured base layer: which channels (position, length, luminance, hue, motion) are discriminable and at what accuracy, and what can be found without serial search — the mechanism behind 'structurally self-announcing'. Targets surveys and replications rather than Tufte-style prescription.
- **attention under concurrent visual load — the exact defect** — `change blindness inattentional blindness operator monitoring display useful field of view peripheral vision attention capture abrupt onset motion transient effect size`
  - The project's stated failure is that a viewer pattern-matching on the globe misses truthful text. This targets the measured magnitude of change/inattentional blindness, exogenous capture by onsets and motion (also why reflowing text steals attention), and how far from fixation anything can be encoded at all — UFOV extents, tunneling under load.
- **quantitative legibility, contrast, and typography thresholds** — `minimum legible character height visual angle arcminutes contrast ratio threshold WCAG 2 versus APCA perceptual contrast validation legibility text over dynamic video background typography research`
  - Yields the designable numbers: angular size minima, luminance-contrast thresholds and whether 4.5:1 was ever validated for glowing text over moving imagery, the APCA critique, x-height/aperture legibility research, 10-foot-UI guidance, and tabular vs proportional figures / digit jitter.
- **glanceable, ambient and peripheral display models plus HUD attention** — `glanceable ambient peripheral display taxonomy evaluation Pousman Stasko Matthews notification interruption cost head-up display cognitive tunneling eyes-off-road glance duration measurement`
  - Closest-fitting body of work to a small chip panel over a scanned scene: how peripheral/ambient displays are evaluated, comprehension-per-glance, interruption cost of transient notices, and automotive/aviation HUD evidence on attention capture and tunneling — also the likeliest home for an information-density-versus-noticing tradeoff with real measurement behind it.
- **contrarian/skeptical — failed replications and boundary conditions** — `chartjunk data-ink ratio empirical test failed replication Miller seven plus minus two misapplied Cowan four working memory Fitts's law gamepad joystick gaze does not transfer boundary conditions`
  - Joseph explicitly wants epistemic tiers kept separate and values a well-evidenced 'this rule does not hold here' over a tidy synthesis. Hunts tested-and-failed prescriptions (Bateman et al. on chartjunk, empirical tests of data-ink), the routine misuse of 7±2 versus Cowan's 4±1, and whether Fitts / Hick–Hyman / the steering law transfer beyond mouse pointing to gamepad, analog stick, or gaze.

### https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27050950/
quality: **primary** · published: 2016 (Journal of Vision 16(2):7; PMID 27050950, doi:10.1167/16.2.7). The fetched PubMed record shows volume/issue/year only — no exact day; J Vis 16(2) is a February 2016 issue.

- **[central]** A concurrent FOVEAL (central visual) task shrinks the useful field of view — the peripheral deficit grows with retinal eccentricity (true tunnel vision) — whereas an auditory working-memory load degrades peripheral sensitivity uniformly across the field without shrinking it. Mechanism, directly on point for a viewer pattern-matching a globe: the operator's own central visual task is what makes an off-axis alarm chip disproportionately hard to detect; an audio/cognitive load alone would not do this.
  > The results showed that although both foveal and auditory loads reduced Gabor orientation sensitivity, only the foveal load interacted with retinal eccentricity to produce tunnel vision, clearly demonstrating task-specific changes to the form of the UFOV.
- **[central]** ANY concurrent load — visual-foveal or auditory-WM — measurably reduces peripheral orientation sensitivity, even when it does not shrink the field. So a peripherally placed status chip loses detectability under load regardless of load modality; only the eccentricity-dependent worsening is modality-specific.
  > although both foveal and auditory loads reduced Gabor orientation sensitivity, only the foveal load interacted with retinal eccentricity
- **[supporting]** Much of the prior useful-field-of-view literature is confounded: it did not control for eccentricity-dependent spatial resolution, so 'tunnel vision' results may reflect fixed retinal/optical properties rather than flexible attentional narrowing. This is an explicit boundary condition on citing older UFOV/tunnel-vision numbers as attention findings.
  > However, most research has not accounted for eccentricity-dependent changes in spatial resolution, potentially conflating fixed visual properties with flexible changes in visual attention.
- **[central]** To equate detectability across the visual field, peripheral stimuli must be scaled up in BOTH size and presentation/processing time — the authors did exactly this to remove the eccentricity confound. Design consequence: an instrument element intended to be equally noticeable off-axis has to be both larger and shown longer than its foveal equivalent; equal pixel size is not equal visibility.
  > Gabor patches were scaled for size and processing time to produce equal performance across the visual field under single-task conditions, thus removing the confound of eccentricity-dependent differences in visual sensitivity.
- **[supporting]** This study adjudicates a genuine dispute in the literature: Williams's (1988, 1989) claim that a foveal load is NECESSARY for tunnel vision is supported, and the competing claim that auditory working-memory load produces tunnel vision is not — the two load types had never before been directly compared for their effect on UFOV size.
  > Williams (1988, 1989) argued that foveal loads are necessary to reduce the size of the UFOV, producing tunnel vision. Without a foveal load, it is argued that the attentional decrement is constant across the visual field (i.e., general interference). However, other research asserts that auditory working memory (WM) loads produce tunnel vision. To date, foveal versus auditory WM loads have not been compared to determine if they differentially change the size of the UFOV.

### https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3897661/
quality: **primary** · published: 2014-01-21

- **[central]** Under a sustained real-time visual monitoring task, 66% of 171 operators failed to detect a salient, clearly visible, ongoing unexpected stimulus that appeared *inside the spatial field of their attentional focus* — i.e. foveal placement and high salience are not sufficient for noticing when the observer is engaged in a demanding concurrent visual task. This is a direct empirical anchor for vivarium's claim that 'a viewer engaged in pattern-matching is not reading the HUD': placing a status chip in the operator's line of sight does not mean it is seen.
  > Based on the post-event measure, 66% of the participants failed to detect salient, ongoing stimuli appearing in the spatial field of their attentional focus.
- **[central]** Task-relevance of the unexpected stimulus is the dominant moderator of whether it is noticed: 79% missed it when it was task-irrelevant (a pirate in costume) versus 55% when task-relevant (a suspicious parcel), χ²(1, N=171)=10.53, p<.001, w=0.25 (small-to-moderate effect). Design implication with a mechanism (attentional set / Most-Simons style expectation filtering): an alarm chip is noticed to the degree it belongs to the semantic and feature set the operator is already searching for — a staleness alarm styled as generic UI chrome is filtered out, whereas one encoded in the same visual vocabulary as the anomalies being hunted survives the filter. Note the boundary condition: even the *relevant* stimulus was still missed by a majority (55%).
  > Supporting _Hypothesis 2,_ participants were less likely to detect the _task-irrelevant_ unexpected stimulus compared to the _task-relevant_ unexpected stimulus, _χ_ 2 (1, _N = 171_) = 10.53, _p_<.001, _w_ = 0.25.
- **[central]** Operator expertise does not inoculate against inattentional blindness: 84 infantry personnel with 1–19 years and a mean of ~250 hours of real CCTV monitoring experience were statistically indistinguishable from 87 naive civilians, χ²(1, N=171)=0.11, p=.746, w=0.02 (near-zero effect). This falsifies the common design assumption that a trained instrument-reader will learn to catch what an untrained one misses — the fix must be structural in the display, not trained into the operator.
  > Prior task experience did not inoculate operators against inattentional blindness effects.
- **[supporting]** The failure is perceptual/attentional, not a memory artifact: real-time verbalization (participants speaking observations into a dictaphone as they watched) showed *higher* miss rates than the post-event questionnaire — 80% (SE=3) overall failed to verbalize detection, 89% (SE=3) for the task-irrelevant event vs 70% (SE=5) for the task-relevant event, χ²(1,171)=9.65, p=.002, w=0.24. This rules out 'inattentional amnesia' (the operator saw it and forgot), meaning the information genuinely never entered the operator's working state — a designed-for-glance panel that is not noticed is not merely un-remembered, it is un-received.
  > Overall, 80% (_SE_ = 3) participants failed to verbalize detection of the unexpected event, and the _task-irrelevant_ event was more likely to go undetected (89%, _SE_ = 3) than the _task-relevant_ event (70%, _SE_ = 5), _χ_ 2 (1, 171) = 9.65, _p_ = .002, _w_ = 0.24.
- **[supporting]** Monitoring duration had no significant effect on inattentional blindness — a ~2-minute clip and a ~43-minute clip (the latter with 17 non-target pedestrians as distractors) produced statistically indistinguishable miss rates, χ²(1, N=171)=0.19, p=.665, w=0.03. This separates inattentional blindness from the classic vigilance decrement: the effect is present from the opening minutes and is not a fatigue phenomenon, so 'the operator has only been watching a short while' is not a defense, and short-session instrument use is not exempt.
  > Clip length was not significantly associated with inattentional blindness, _χ_ 2 (1, _N = 171_) = 0.19, _p_ = .665

### https://cognitiveresearchjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41235-021-00312-2
quality: **primary** · published: 2021-06-26

- **[central]** Background/distractor motion measurably delays detection of a feature change in a concurrently-monitored item: mean RT to detect a 30° orientation change was 439 ms (SD 58) in stationary displays vs 519 ms (SD 69) in moving displays — an ~80 ms cost with a very large effect size, F(1,27) = 97.52, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.783 (Exp. 1, N = 30, Gabor patches, set sizes 1–8, item speeds 85–254 px/s). This is the closest empirical analogue to a status readout that must be noticed while the operator scans a moving globe.
  > the addition of moving distractors slowed participants' response time to detect an orientation changes in a moving target
- **[central]** Motion of the changing element itself is a SECOND, independent source of detection delay, additive to the surrounding-motion cost: in mixed displays (half static, half moving), detection of a change on a moving target was 43 ms slower than on a static target (623 ms vs 580 ms, p = 0.011); full ordering was pure-static 544 ms < mixed-static 580 ms < mixed-moving 623 ms < pure-moving 635 ms, F(3,84) = 25.53, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.477 (Exp. 2, N = 31). Design consequence: an alarm/status chip should itself be motionless even when everything around it moves — the two disruptions do not collapse into one.
  > motion possessed by the target item itself and in surrounding items are two independent sources of disruption to the detection of the change event
- **[central]** Display density and motion interact multiplicatively rather than additively: RT rose with set size at a static search slope of 9.7 ms/item (2–8 items), F(6,162) = 23.24, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.463, and the motion penalty was LARGER at larger set sizes. This is a measured instance of the information-density-versus-noticing tradeoff — each additional monitored element costs detection latency, and the cost per element is inflated when the scene moves.
  > search slope from 2 to 8 items was 9.7 ms per item for the static display
- **[supporting]** The authors attribute the effect to 'motion silencing' (Suchow & Alvarez 2011), under which feature changes on moving objects are perceptually attenuated — and they extend it from hue/luminance/size/shape to orientation. This names a mechanism, not just an association: the same nominal change signal is weaker to the observer when it rides on motion, so encoding critical state as a change-in-appearance on or near moving content is mechanistically disadvantaged.
  > objects changing in hue, luminance, size and shape appear to change less rapidly when they move
- **[supporting]** BOUNDARY CONDITION limiting how far this transfers: the study measured response time ONLY — every trial contained a change, there were no target-absent trials, no response deadline, and no miss-rate or accuracy data are reported. It therefore establishes a latency cost (tens of ms) under motion, and does NOT establish that motion causes outright misses of a change, nor bound the magnitude of any such miss rate. Applied guidance the authors do draw is workstation-level, not pixel-level.
  > A consideration for current practices in CCTV monitoring, for example, is to limit the number of screens being monitored by each operator.

### https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10533
quality: **primary** · published: 2022-12-20 (arXiv v1; v2 2022-12-21). Published version: IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2022.3226463 (early access 2022, TVCG 2023).

- **[central]** In a replication of Cleveland & McGill's graphical comparison experiment, the expected performance difference between individual people (1.5–3 percentage points) is LARGER than the expected performance difference between the pie, bubble, and stacked-bar encodings themselves (1–1.5 percentage points) — i.e. who is reading the display matters more than which of those encodings you chose. This directly bounds how much design authority a canonical encoding ranking can carry.
  > Results show that expected differences in performance across pie, bubble, and stacked-bar charts (1-1.5pp) is smaller than the expected differences between people (1.5-3pp).
- **[central]** The canonical graphical-perception ranking (position > area > angle > volume) is a property of the 'average observer' model, not of individuals: a credible fraction of people show accuracy patterns that deviate from it. The ranking is therefore an aggregate summary statistic, not a per-viewer prediction — a boundary condition on one of the most-cited results in visualization design.
  > Graphical perception studies typically measure visualization encoding effectiveness using the error of an 'average observer', leading to canonical rankings of encodings for numerical attributes: e.g., position > area > angle > volume. Yet different people may vary in their ability to read different visualization types, leading to variance in this ranking across individuals not captured by population-level metrics using 'average observer' models. [...] The results from experiments and modeling indicate that some people show patterns of accuracy that credibly deviate from the canonical rankings of visualization effectiveness.
- **[central]** Quantified deviation rate: roughly 20–25% of people consistently perform better with a chart type other than the population-best (bar), and fewer than 40% of people are expected to hold the canonical bar-best / pie-second ordering. These are checkable proportions a designer can plan against rather than a qualitative caveat.
  > While Bar charts elicit the best performance on average, results show that around 20-25% of people consistently perform better with another chart type. [...] Less than 40% of people are expected to share the 'canonical' ranking of Bar best and Pie as second-best, with some instead performing better with Stacked Bar or Bubble.
- **[supporting]** The authors argue the standard psychophysical explanation for encoding-effectiveness differences has been overstated, because individual-difference variance can exceed the mean effect of chart type. This is a claim about causal attribution, not just noise: the mechanism usually invoked (perceptual channel accuracy) explains less of the observed error structure than assumed.
  > The variation in performance between individuals across different chart types may imply that the role of psychophysical explanations for differences in error rates has been overstated...the effects of variance across individuals can dwarf the mean effects of chart type.
- **[supporting]** Methodological prescription with an epistemic-tier consequence: the paper recommends reporting effect sizes with their uncertainty (in units translatable to practice) instead of rankings and log-error metrics, so designers can weigh the cost/benefit of optimizing an encoding. Study basis: 109 participants after exclusions, 130,800 judgments, bar/pie/bubble/stacked-bar, Bayesian multilevel regression; population-level ordering did broadly replicate prior work with bar lowest-error.
  > In this paper we replicate and extend Cleveland and McGill's graphical comparison experiment using Bayesian multilevel regression, using these models to explore individual differences in visualization skill from multiple perspectives. [...] We discuss implications of these findings, such as a need for new ways to communicate visualization effectiveness to designers

### https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/healey/PP/
quality: **primary** · published: 2012 (IEEE TVCG 18(7):1170–1188); the companion web page is undated/continuously maintained and states only that "An extended version of this web page has been accepted for publication in IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics."

- **[central]** There is an operational, measurable definition of "structurally self-announcing": a visual property is preattentive iff detection time on a large multi-element display stays under ~200–250 ms AND is flat with respect to the number of distractors (set-size independence). The 200 ms figure is anchored to a physiological floor — eye movements themselves take at least 200 ms to initiate — so a genuinely preattentive cue is detected before the operator can even saccade to it. This is a falsifiable design test for a status chip or staleness alarm: instrument the search-time-vs-clutter slope; if it rises with the number of other chips on screen, the encoding is not preattentive and the alarm can be missed.
  > Tasks that can be performed on large multi-element displays in less than 200 to 250 milliseconds (msec) are considered preattentive. ... Eye movements take at least 200 msec to initiate ... If task completion time is relatively constant and below some chosen threshold, independent of the number of distractors, the task is said to be preattentive.
- **[central]** Postattentive vision: prior study of a display confers essentially no search advantage on it later. After a viewer has attended to an object and looked away, its preattentive representation is identical to the pre-study representation, and conjunction search still costs a serial ~20–50 ms per object. This directly refutes the common design defense that an operator will "learn the panel" and thereafter read it cheaply — a text block that must be searched costs per-element scan time on every glance, forever. Falsifiable prediction for vivarium: familiarity with the debug dump's layout will not reduce the time to find a changed field in it.
  > The preattentive visual representation of an object *after* a viewer studies it and looks at something else appears to be identical to its representation *before* the viewer studied it. ... viewers continued to require 20-50 msec per object to complete the search. ... In most cases, visualization displays are novel, and their contents cannot be committed to long-term memory. This means that studying a display may offer no assistance in searching for specific data values.
- **[central]** Visual features form an asymmetric interference hierarchy, not a set of independent channels: background hue variation destroys the ability to see shape/form patterns and texture patterns, while shape and texture variation do not degrade hue-pattern detection; luminance similarly dominates hue (Callaghan's luminance-on-hue preference). The asymmetry is the operational point — it means chrome laid over a hue-varied moving globe loses form/shape encodings first, and that the corrective is to encode critical state in the dominant channel (luminance) rather than in shape or hue against a colored background.
  > Background variations in colour interfere with a viewer's ability to identify the presence of individual shapes and the spatial patterns they form. If colour is held constant across the display, these same shape patterns are immediately visible. The interference is asymmetric: random variations in shape have no effect on a viewer's ability to see colour patterns. ... Callaghan also documented a luminance-on-hue preference during her experiments ... a hue-on-texture interference has been shown to exist; random variations in hue interfere with the identification of texture patterns, but not vice-versa.
- **[central]** Motion is the strongest change-signal available and it works in the periphery, where colour and shape changes do not — applying motion to a static glyph was significantly easier to detect than changing its colour or shape, both at the centre of focus and in the periphery (RTs ~200–310 ms foveal, ~200–350 ms peripheral). But motion types are not interchangeable in cost: ranked by distraction, flicker is least distracting, then oscillating motion, then divergence, then movement over long distances. This gives a designable ordering for an unmissable staleness alarm — and it indicts in-place text reflow, which is precisely "movement over long distances" (the most distracting category) applied to non-critical content. Also designable: peripheral flicker needs ~100% or more frequency variation to be discriminable versus 2–5% at the centre of focus, so a peripheral flicker alarm must be near-binary, not a subtle rate change.
  > Applying motion to a static glyph was significantly easier to recognized, compared to changing the glyph's color or shape. This finding held both when the glyph was near the center of focus, and when it was located on the periphery of the viewer's gaze. ... Flicker was the least distracting, followed by oscillating motion, then divergence, and finally movement over long distances. ... Frequency must vary from 2-5% to produce a distinguishable difference in flicker at the center of focus (1.02 ≤ ΔF ≤ 1.05) ... 100% or more for distinguishable difference in flicker in the periphery
- **[central]** Change blindness has a measured magnitude large enough to invalidate "the change was on screen" as evidence that an operator saw it: in the Levin & Simons actor-substitution study nearly two-thirds of subjects failed to report that the main actor had been replaced, and 70% of those who missed it subsequently described the character using details from the *original* actor — i.e. they confidently reported a stale percept as current. The effect is not confined to unattended objects; changes to attended objects are also missed when unexpected. This is the literature's formalization of vivarium's own finding that a truth living only in a text panel is not "said" to an operator who is pattern-matching on the globe.
  > An interruption in what is being seen (_e.g.,_ a blink, an eye saccade, or a blank screen) renders us 'blind' to significant changes that occur in the scene during the interruption. ... Nearly two-thirds of the subjects failed to report that the main actor was replaced ... 70% of the subjects who failed to see the change described the central character using details from the initial actor, and not the replacement ... Changes to attended objects can also be missed, particularly when the changes are unexpected

### https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15327051hci1904_10
quality: **primary** · published: 2004-12-01

- **[central]** Changes to a visual display are frequently missed when they coincide with a visual transient — Durlach names eye blinks, screen flashes, and scene relocation as the masking events. This is directly load-bearing for vivarium: a reflowing/redrawing text overlay is a local screen flash, and a moving/rotating globe is continuous scene relocation, so a status value that changes during camera motion or during a panel reflow is in the regime the literature says will be missed. ACCESS CAVEAT: abstract only — tandfonline returned 403 and no full text was obtainable, so no effect sizes or paradigm-level numbers from this paper were verified.
  > Recent research on change detection suggests that people often fail to notice changes in visual displays when they occur at the same time as various forms of visual transients, including eye blinks, screen flashes, and scene relocation.
- **[central]** Detection failure is worst specifically when the observer's attention is drawn away from the location where the change occurs — i.e. spatial attention allocation, not display presence, governs whether a change is seen. This formalizes the project's own stated intuition that 'a viewer engaged in pattern-matching is not reading the HUD': an operator scanning the globe has attention away from the panel, which is the precise condition under which a panel-local change is not registered.
  > Distractions that draw the observer's attention away from the location of the change especially lead to detection failure.
- **[central]** Durlach's stated top-level design implication is that change detection should be engineered into the system as a dedicated tool rather than delegated to the operator's memory and attention — i.e. the remedy is instrumentation, not vigilance or training. For a freshness/staleness alarm this means the system must itself compute 'this changed' and announce it structurally; presenting the current value truthfully and expecting the operator to notice it differ from the last value is the failure mode this paper names.
  > The major implication is that systems should provide users with dedicated change-detection tools, instead of leaving change detection to the vagaries of human memorial and attentional processes.
- **[supporting]** Durlach explicitly transfers the laboratory change-blindness result to the operational monitoring-and-control domain, asserting that operationally important information changes can be missed in complex visual displays under transients or distraction — this is the citation that connects the basic-perception literature to instrument-panel design rather than leaving it as a lab curiosity.
  > As process monitoring and control systems rely on humans interacting with complex visual displays, there is a possibility that important changes in visually presented information will be missed if the changes occur coincident with a visual transient or distraction.
- **[supporting]** Epistemic tier of this source: it is a review/position article synthesizing prior change-detection experiments, not a report of new measurements — its own framing is 'review research ... and discuss its implications'. It should therefore be cited as a principled bridge to the operational domain and as a route to the underlying primary experiments, not as an independent empirical result with an effect size.
  > The purpose of this article is to review research on so called "change blindness" and discuss its implications for the design of visual interfaces for complex monitoring and control systems.

### https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40029311/
quality: **primary** · published: 2025-03 (Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 51(3), 283–299; doi 10.1037/xhp0001275; PMID 40029311; free full text PMC11908675)

- **[central]** Abrupt onsets (the sudden appearance of a new object) are a privileged class of attention-capturing stimulus: in the founding Yantis & Jonides experiments only onset targets produced shallow visual-search slopes, while luminance-singleton and color-singleton targets produced steep slopes — i.e. brightness or color alone did not capture attention the way an onset did. Design implication: for an alarm that must be unmissable over a scanned scene, the capturing event is the *arrival of a new element*, not a recolor or a brightening of an element already present. This is (b) empirically established and replicated, with the review's own summary that onsets remain potent 'even after controlling for differences in luminance and color'.
  > only abruptly onset targets produced a shallow search slope, while both luminance and color conditions produced steep search slopes ... abrupt onsets are more powerful at capturing attention compared to color singletons ... abrupt onsets continued to capture attention, even after controlling for differences in luminance and color
- **[central]** Onset capture survives the learned/history-based suppression that successfully defeats color singletons. In a paradigm where the distractor was never the target across trials (no incentive whatever to attend it) and target/distractor features were held constant to maximize learning, color-singleton distractors were effectively ignored but abrupt onsets kept capturing attention. Falsifiable and directly load-bearing: an operator cannot train themselves out of onset capture the way they can train themselves out of a static color cue — which makes an onset the right primitive for a rare must-see alarm, and makes any persistently onsetting chrome (a reflowing text block, a re-laid-out panel) a permanent, non-habituatable tax on the operator's attention.
  > unlike the irrelevant-singleton paradigm used in Yantis and Jonides' work, in the current task, the distractor was never the target, eliminating any incentive to attend to the distractor. The results show that, although color-singleton distractors were effectively ignored, abrupt onsets continued to capture attention.
- **[central]** Capture by onsets is defeatable but never fully eliminated by top-down focus: a 100%-valid spatial cue delivered 200 ms before the display 'virtually eliminated' the onset effect, yet the residual was near-zero *and consistently positive in every experiment* — which the authors read as capture still occurring followed by rapid disengagement, not as capture being prevented. Boundary condition that cuts both ways for a scanning operator: (i) an operator deeply engaged in pattern-matching one region of the globe will show much-attenuated response to a peripheral chip, so onset alone is not a guarantee of noticing; (ii) conversely, no chrome placed anywhere can be made truly cost-free, because even perfectly-predicted onsets still cost a small consistent amount.
  > a valid and temporally useful cue (a 100% valid cue appearing 200 ms before the item display) virtually eliminated any effect of abrupt onsets ... in Yantis and Jonides (1990), cueing the target location in advance resulted in near-zero but consistently positive effects of abrupt onsets in all experiments.
- **[central]** Capture strength is modulated by how often the onset fires: Folk & Remington (2015) found that onset cues presented on only 20% of trials produced capture effects, while onset cues presented on 100% of trials did not. ### https://perso.telecom-paristech.fr/rioul/publis/202001liugoririoulbeaudouinlafonguiard.pdf
quality: **primary** · published: 2020-04-25 (ACM CHI 2020 proceedings; conference April 25–30, 2020, Honolulu, HI; DOI 10.1145/3313831.3376878)

- **[central]** In real HCI tasks, Hick's-law slope is so small that choice-reaction time is effectively constant in the number of alternatives: the authors' re-analysis of two published command-selection datasets (Roy et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2017) yields slopes of 32 ms/bit, 8 ms/bit and 4 ms/bit — i.e. going from 2 to 16 on-screen items costs on the order of 12–96 ms of decision latency, not a design-relevant amount. Falsifiable, designable consequence: adding a few more status chips to an instrument panel does not measurably slow a trained operator's decision time, and any budget derived from Hick's law for such a panel is spurious.
  > The slope of Hick’s law is very small: 32ms/bit in the Glass condition, 8ms/bit in the Glass+Skin condition and 4ms/bit for command selection [26]. ... In practice, reaction time RT can usually be treated as a constant.
- **[central]** The popular "Hick-based" design principle ("less is better" — minimize visible choices, split them into categories) is not merely unsupported by Hick's law but mathematically contradicted by it: because the logarithm is concave, applying Hick's law to N=32 items gives a+5b for one page versus 2.5a+7.5b for four uncategorized pages and 2a+5b for four categorized pages, so the log model recommends showing everything at once. The authors generalize this to a convexity criterion — if the latency function f is convex (e.g. quadratic, as in pairwise comparison of items) grouping reduces latency; if f is concave (logarithmic) display all items at once; each extra nesting level costs an additional (m−1)a seconds of fixed overhead.
  > As a result, the optimal strategy according to Hick’s law consists of displaying all the items at once on the same page, contrary to the design principle that choices should be categorized. ... Hence, the design principle cannot be justified by Hick’s law, nor by any other logarithmic RL function.
- **[supporting]** Observing logarithmic growth of a time measure against the number of items is not evidence for Hick's law — many unrelated mechanisms produce a logarithm (visual search in a hierarchical menu per Landauer & Nachbar 1985; scrolling in an alphabetically ordered menu per Cockburn & Gutwin 2009; any divide-and-conquer strategy). The authors name this "conflation between the formula and the law" as the dominant misuse in HCI, and document it across a table of eight studies (Landauer & Nachbar, Cockburn et al., Soukoreff & MacKenzie, MacKenzie et al., Wobbrock & Myers, Ruiz et al.) whose tasks were visual search, decision, or mode switching rather than the stimulus-response choice-reaction paradigm Hick measured.
  > attributing them to Hick’s law is a very unfortunate conflation between the formula and the law. ... Therefore observing a logarithmic relationship is not sufficient to attribute the underlying phenomenon to Hick’s law.
- **[supporting]** The slope of Hick's law is a property of stimulus-response compatibility and training, not a fixed human constant: Hick's own light-and-key task gave about 200 ms/bit, whereas tasks with very high S-R compatibility (pressing the finger that feels a vibration — Leonard 1959; reading Arabic numerals aloud — Mowbray 1960) give slopes approaching 0 ms/bit; practice drops slopes from 73 to 23 ms/bit over two months (Knight & Dagnall 1967), and Seibel (1963) found essentially no reaction-time difference between 31 (5-bit) and 1,023 (10-bit) alternatives after more than 75,000 trials. Because HCI tasks are near-perfectly S-R compatible (respond to exactly the item that lit up), the effect is expected to be near zero by construction.
  > whereas Hick’s light and key experiment reports rates of about 200 ms/bit, there are many cases where the slope approaches 0 ms/bit (Fig. 1). ... In such cases, which correspond to extremely high levels of S-R compatibility, there is virtually no effect of stimulus uncertainty on reaction time.
- **[supporting]** Hick's law has a measured validity envelope rather than universal scope: linearity of reaction time in stimulus entropy has been established consistently only over roughly 1 to 3–4 bits (about 2 to 16 equiprobable alternatives); above that the law over-estimates reaction time, and Fitts & Posner report that regardless of the number of alternatives reaction time seldom exceeds 1 s. The envelope is task-dependent — Pollack found linearity extending to about 10 bits in a word-naming task — and the law under-estimates reaction time once error rates rise (Fitts: beyond 0.6 bits of equivocation, information lost to errors grows faster than the speed gain).

However, whenever the number of choices becomes larger, it seems that reaction time is consistently over-estimated. In fact, Fitts & Posner [16] indicated that whatever the number of possible stimuli, reaction time will seldom exceed 1s. ... The actual range where the relationship holds is thus very dependent on the actual task.
