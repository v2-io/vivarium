**Grading key** (Joseph's three-tier scheme, applied per claim, using the corpus's own tier language where it already supplied one):
- **[T]** theoretically principled — a stated mechanism/derivation, not (yet, or ever) measured
- **[E]** empirically established — measured, with population/context noted; I flag single-study vs. reviewed/replicated
- **[C]** craft consensus — asserted, widely practiced, no measurement behind it
- Compound tags (**[T+E]**, **[C→E]**) mean the source itself spans tiers or upgrades one to the other

Where a claim has a **gloss** — a "the source says X, and concludes Y-about-vivarium" structure — I split them: the **fact** line is what the source actually says, the *→ inference* line is the extrapolation, graded separately.

## 1. The two questions you flagged, answered directly

### Q1 — Is there a principled, measured account of how much a panel may display before it effectively displays nothing?

**Short answer: no general one exists, and two of the corpus's own reviews say so explicitly.** What exists instead is a set of domain-specific proxies, none of which is quite the number you're picturing:

- The **2015 Human Factors clutter review** (§4.4 below) states outright that ergonomics has **no established quantitative density ceiling** — "the challenge is to determine the ideal middle ground between excessive data and insufficient information" is presented as an *open* problem, not a solved one. **[T]**, reviewed literature, 2015.
- The **2019 TVCG dashboard-taxonomy paper** (§6.1) makes the same admission from the visualization side: its only citation for "more disaggregated data helps high-uncertainty tasks, but showing detail always hurts accuracy" is a single 1979 lab study in accounting information systems (Benbasat & Dexter) — a 40-year-old, off-domain result standing in for a modern density ceiling. **[C]**, weak citation chain, flagged as a genuine gap by the paper's own authors.
- What **does** exist as a hard number is domain-specific to *alarms*, not displays generally: EEMUA 191's alarm-rate ceilings (§4.1) — but the two secondary sources reporting it disagree with each other on the actual number (flagged as a live discrepancy below, §9).
- **NUREG-0700 Rev. 3** gives the closest thing to a general display-density number: guideline 1.5-8 caps packing density at **50% overall, 25% for alphanumeric-heavy displays**, with the instruction to *minimize* (not just cap) density for critical information. But its own provenance code traces to Smith & Mosier's 1986 NASA/MITRE handbook — this is **[C]** codified into a standard, not an experimentally derived threshold. Treat it as usable, not as evidence.
- The most *methodologically* interesting answer is **Rosenholtz et al.'s image-computable clutter metrics** (§4.3): Feature Congestion, Subband Entropy, and Edge Density take a raw screenshot and return a continuous clutter score that correlates with visual search performance — i.e., a monotonic cost function rather than a threshold. This is genuinely **[E]**, but the corpus could not retrieve the correlation magnitudes (paywalled), so there's no knee-point to cite, only a validated methodology for computing one yourself. **Actionable**: you could literally run this measure against `vivarium explore` screenshots before/after a HUD redesign — Feature Congestion is explicitly defined as "how hard would it be to add a new item that reliably draws attention," which is exactly your alarm-salience question, formalized.
- The **best-supported qualitative answer**, converging from three independent, non-citing literatures (aviation, games, dashboards): **it isn't a count or a percentage, it's whether task-critical elements get drowned out.** Caroux 2022's four-experiment game study (§7.4) found HUD *size* and *color* changes produced near-zero effects (η²p < .01) while removing *task-relevant* elements produced huge ones (η²p = .41–.67) — density itself wasn't the variable that mattered, relevance was. Few's dashboard heuristics (§6.2) and the TVCG dashboard paper's criticality-ordering rule (§6.1) converge on the same instinct from a completely different, non-citing literature. That's real convergence, even though none of these sources cite each other — worth treating as more reliable than any single one of them.

**Bottom line for vivarium**: don't chase a percentage. If you want a *number*, NUREG-0700's 50%/25% is defensible-but-inherited, and Rosenholtz's clutter metrics are a genuinely measurable instrument you could apply to your own screenshots. If you want the *design move*, the corpus's real answer is: rank by criticality, not by count, and validate that the freshness alarm and mode chip survive Rosenholtz-style clutter scoring against the busiest globe frame you expect to render.

### Q2 — When an instrument must announce its own unreliability (stale/degraded/placeholder), what actually works?

This is where the corpus is richest, and where I found the most genuinely surprising convergence. Pulling the threads together (full citations in §5 and §2 below):

**The strongest, most-corroborated finding in the whole corpus**: an *unannounced wrong value is a worse defect class than an absent one*, and this shows up independently in three literatures that do not cite each other:
- **Aviation** (FAA AC 25-11B, §2.4): hazard-classification tables rank *misleading* display strictly above *loss* of the same display (Catastrophic vs. Major-Hazardous) — the only thing that demotes "misleading" back down is if a monitor auto-detects the fault and tells the crew.
- **Nuclear** (NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, §5.3): requires instrument/sensor failures to produce "distinct display changes" and imposes a four-state validity taxonomy — **valid / invalid / unvalidated / numerical estimate** — refusing to collapse "known bad" and "not checkable" into one state.
- **Visualization/dashboards** (TVCG 2019, §6.1): field studies found the dashboard *form itself* manufactures unearned credibility — people read dashboard data as "trustworthy and definitive" *because* the visual organization signals objectivity, independent of whether the data is actually good. This means a well-designed status-chip strip could make a stale value look **more** trustworthy than the current wall-of-text does — raising the stakes on getting the freshness alarm right, not lowering them.

**The mechanism that actually works, per the regulatory/handbook sources, is structural, not textual**: the invalidity indication must occupy the *same location* as the value it questions (AC 25-11B §6.2.1.7: "failure flags should be presented in the location of the information they reference or replace"), and it must be a *distinct display change*, not a caveat elsewhere (NUREG 14.2-2). Two more mechanisms worth knowing about, from different corners of the corpus:
- **Graded/likelihood-coded alerting** (Sorkin, Kantowitz & Kantowitz 1988, §2.1): encode the confidence *in-band on the alert itself* rather than in a separate readout. The genuinely useful finding — small, dated (1988, lab, dual-task), but directly on point — is that this did **not** measurably increase attentional load, and the likelihood level entered operators' decisions rather than functioning only as a trigger. **[E]**, one lab study.
- **Legibility-degradation encoding** (Padilla, Kay & Hullman 2022, citing Correll/Moritz/Heer, §5.2): make the value itself *harder to read* in proportion to its uncertainty — blur proportional to positional error, or hue-desaturation toward gray as confidence drops, so the most uncertain values become indistinguishable. Measured to shift how much weight viewers give uncertainty vs. a standard bivariate color map, at a real cost to point-lookup precision. This is a genuinely different mechanism class from an annunciator — it's the datum announcing its own unreliability by becoming less legible, rather than a separate flag doing the announcing.

**A distinction the corpus doesn't state explicitly but that falls out of combining two of its threads, and that I think is the single most useful synthesis-level point for your specific staleness problem:** Ecological Interface Design's core mechanism (§3) is making *constraint violations* perceptually self-evident — a bad sensor reading becomes visible because it breaks a physical law the display makes legible. That mechanism is powerful for **wrong-and-internally-inconsistent** data (a value that violates the world's own physics), but it does nothing for **wrong-but-internally-consistent** data — which is exactly what a stale cache is. A world that stopped updating three minutes ago still looks completely lawful; nothing in it contradicts anything else in it. EID's constraint-violation machinery is structurally blind to staleness by construction. That means vivarium's freshness alarm cannot be designed as "let violations become visible" — it needs its own explicit clock-based annunciator regardless of how good the physics-legibility of the rest of the display gets. This is worth stating plainly in the segment: EID answers "how do I show a sensor is lying," not "how do I show a sensor stopped talking."

**Two corroborating findings from completely different domains converge on "don't rely on a transient/textual cue":**
- FAA AC 25-11B §5.11.3.4, verbatim: "a text change by itself should not be used as an attention-getting cue." **[T, regulatory guidance, not itself put to adversarial vote]** — note this is a *different* FAA document from 14 CFR 25.1322 (§2.3), which *was* voted; I'm not claiming this specific claim inherited that vote's confidence, only noting the general caution from §2.3 (regulation quotes tend to be genuine, glosses tend to overreach) is worth applying here too before treating this as settled.
- Caroux & Isbister 2016 (§7.6), eye-tracking 15 players across two commercial games: "transient / non-permanent HUD elements... drew very few eye fixations compared to permanently displayed elements." **[E]**, small sample (n=15), authors themselves call it too sparse to draw strong conclusions — but it is measured, independent of the aviation regulation, and points the same direction.

That's two independent literatures (federal aviation rulemaking; games eye-tracking) that don't cite each other, converging on: **a toast/confirmation-style transient message is a weak channel for anything that must be noticed.** Directly relevant to your plan to move critical state into stable chips and demote transient stuff to toasts — the corpus supports doing exactly that split, but not relying on the toast channel for anything load-bearing.

**One risk worth naming for the specific threshold you'll pick**: Padilla/Kay/Hullman (§5.2) also report that any hard visual boundary — an error-bar cap, a threshold color-band — gets read by viewers as *categorically* meaningful, subconsciously and instruction-resistant, even when the underlying quantity is continuous and the boundary is somewhat arbitrary. If you pick "stale after N seconds" and render it as a hard color flip, expect people to treat N as physically meaningful even if you know it's a somewhat arbitrary choice.

## 2. Flight-deck alerting, regulation, and mode awareness

### 2.1 Sorkin, Kantowitz & Kantowitz (1988) — likelihood alarm displays
*Source: sagepub 10.1177/001872088803000406 — **primary**, published 1988-08. Full text paywalled (403); recovered via corroborated secondary retrieval of the abstract.*

- **[E]**, one lab dual-task study. LAD = an alert whose signal code carries the automated monitor's confidence estimate in-band. Canonical 1988 formalization of graded (vs. binary) alerting — direct human-factors precedent for "how does an instrument announce its own uncertainty." → *vivarium inference (mine, not the source's): a freshness/confidence chip could be graded rather than binary, following this precedent* — reasonable but untested at vivarium's scale.
- **[E]** LADs did **not** necessarily increase attentional load — counters the craft intuition that a richer alert costs more to process.
- **[E]** Likelihood level entered operators' decision process and improved attention *allocation* across concurrent tasks — evidence a graded chip functions as reasoned-with evidence, not just a trigger.
- **[E]** Performance improved on *both* primary and secondary task under automated monitoring — not a zero-sum draw on attention in this paradigm.
- **[E]** Tested in two channels (color-coded visual + synthetic speech), both worked — the graded-alerting result isn't tied to one sensory modality. Caveat: 1988 lab dual-task participants, not field operators; population/context is thin.

### 2.2 FAA AC 25-11B (2014-10-07)
*Source: faa.gov advisory circular — **primary**. This is guidance, not binding regulation, but it's the FAA's own design-acceptable-means-of-compliance document — heavily quoted with clause numbers.*

- **[T, regulatory]** §5.11.3.4: "a text change by itself should not be used as an attention-getting cue (for example, to annunciate mode changes)." §5.11.2 requires fixed-location annunciation, with anything needing immediate awareness in the crew's forward/primary field of view. §5.11.4 bounds blink escalation quantitatively: 0.8–4.0 Hz, cancel-means required if blinking persists beyond ~10s. → *Direct regulatory endorsement of a fixed-position chip strip over a reflowing text dump — this is close to a direct citation for the redesign's central premise.*
- **[T, regulatory]** §4.6.7 requires staleness/validity checks for safety-critical parameters "where possible," defines "Coasting Data" (C.12) as data not updated for a defined period, and §6.2.1.7 requires failure flags "in the location of the information they reference or replace." §4.6.8 requires alert latency to be bounded by criticality and required crew response time.
- **[T, regulatory]** Clutter is defined by *cost* (§5.10.3.1: "increased flightcrew processing time for display interpretation"), not appearance. §6.3.3.1 gives a six-part admissibility test for hiding information part-time (not continuously needed; auto-displays when abnormal or relevant to a failure; manually selectable without interference; fails safe per §25.1309; doesn't create unacceptable clutter on arrival; must be accompanied by alerting if its arrival isn't self-evident). §5.8.3 caps parameter color-coding at six colors. → *This is the closest thing in the whole corpus to a principled "what may be moved to an on-demand dump" rule, and it directly licenses vivarium's plan under named conditions* — worth quoting the six-part test verbatim in the segment if this becomes load-bearing.
- **[T, regulatory]** Table 4-1/4-7 hazard classification: *misleading* attitude/engine display ranks Catastrophic, *loss* of the same ranks Major-Hazardous; Appendix C.66 demotes "misleading" back to non-misleading only if a monitor auto-detects the fault and annunciates it. This is the aviation leg of the three-literature convergence in §1/Q2 above.
- **[T, regulatory]** §5.9.1 bounds rather than bans smoothing: "filtering or coasting of data intended to smooth the motion of display elements should not introduce significant positioning errors or create system lag." §5.11.5.3 extends the same logic to imagery: "image failure, freezing, coasting, or color changes should not be misleading." Numbers given: update rate ≥15 Hz for manual-control parameters, attitude display lag ≤100 ms (first-order equivalent time constant). → *This is directly relevant to vivarium's no-depiction-without-referent norm — aviation's version of the same constraint isn't a ban, it's "smoothing may not displace the depicted value or its timing," which is close to what your norm already says, stated with numbers attached.*

### 2.3 14 CFR 25.1322 (eCFR, current text; Amdt. 25-131, 2010-11-02)
*Source: ecfr.gov — **primary**, binding federal regulation. **Three of this source's five claims were put to adversarial vote and all three came back contested or refuted** — not because the quotes were fake (all were verified verbatim against Cornell LII and govinfo mirrors) but because the extracted claim's *gloss* overreached what the regulation actually says. This pattern, and its implication for the two claims below that were never voted on, is worth reading as a unit — see the "Not voted on" note just below.*

Voted (verified pool, summarized from the corpus's own vote records at the end of the source file):

- (c)(2) two-sense requirement — **contested, core survives**: quote genuine and the two-channel requirement for Warning/Caution is real, but "decades ago" is wrong (2010 rule) and the claim's scope-inflation to "a truth existing only as text is never said" oversteps — Advisory tier carries no two-sense requirement.
- (d)(2) suppression must be unmistakably annunciated — **contested, split**: quote genuine, but the same section's (c)(3) explicitly permits *silent* acknowledgment/suppression of ordinary attention-getting cues. (d)(2) is scoped specifically to failure of the *alerting function itself* being suppressed, not to routine operator acknowledgment. Don't over-read this as "no silent state changes ever."

**Not voted on** (unverified — treat the gloss with the same suspicion as its three voted siblings above):
- **[T, regulatory]** (a)(2)/(a)(3): simultaneous multiple alerts named as an explicit certification load case ("readily and easily detectable... including conditions where multiple alerts are provided"); alerts must be removed when the condition no longer exists. → *vivarium inference in the claim as extracted: "a persistent banner outliving its trigger is a compliance defect, not merely untidy" — this inference is plausible and probably survives (it's a direct reading of "(a)(3) requires alerts to be removed"), but given the source's 3-for-3 gloss-overreach record, I'd verify the exact subclause numbering before quoting it as a citation rather than an inference.*
- **[T, regulatory]** (e)(1)/(e)(2): color is a reserved namespace — red=warning, amber/yellow=caution, advisory may use "any color except red or green" (green withheld even though it isn't itself an alert color, protecting its non-alert meaning); monochromatic displays must substitute another visual dimension. → *This one reads as a fairly literal restatement of the clause with less interpretive reach than the other four — lower overreach risk, but still unvoted.*

### 2.4 Nikolic & Sarter (2001) — peripheral vs. foveal automation feedback
*Source: sagepub 10.1518/001872001775992525 — **primary**, Human Factors 43(1), 2001. Full text paywalled; abstract-level only.*

- One claim from this source **was** voted and **REFUTED**: "peripheral displays did not degrade concurrent task performance" — not because it's false, but because the extrapolation to "measured refutation" of an attention-cost tradeoff couldn't be checked against the paywalled method, and Semantic Scholar's citation-context corpus surfaced a competing finding from the same lineage (pilots missed >10% of FMS mode changes, worse under competing attention demand) that the claim's framing erased.
- **[E]**, same paywall/abstract-only caveat applies to these unvoted siblings — treat with the same caution:
  - Two peripheral-feedback implementations for uncommanded automation-status changes both beat the current foveal feedback (Flight Mode Annunciator) on detection rate and response time, in a piloted flight-sim study.
  - **[T]** The paper's causal thesis: foveal-only feedback structurally can't support tracking system-induced change in parallel with a concurrent task — i.e., information requiring fixation is, for a monitoring operator, effectively unsaid. → *If this holds, it's a direct 2001 precedent for vivarium's own "attention is part of instrument honesty" framing — worth knowing this isn't a novel articulation, it restates a human-factors result.*
  - **[T]** Named hazard class: high autonomy + low observability — a system changing state without operator command, where the change isn't self-announcing. Directly names vivarium's silent-stale-cache failure mode.
  - **[C]**, authors' own stated generalization (not measured): transfer beyond aviation to process control/medicine is *asserted*, not tested — the actual data is aviation-only, pilots-only.

### 2.5 Sarter & Woods (1995) — mode awareness
*Source: sagepub 10.1518/001872095779049516 — **secondary** recovery (SAGE 403'd, abstract elided by publisher too; recovered via TRID bibliographic record + a secondary summary site). 985 citations per Semantic Scholar — canonical, not marginal, but this extraction is two steps removed from the primary text.*

- **[T]** Mode proliferation has a cognitive cost that must be paid in the interface — adding functions/options isn't free; designers who multiply modes without growing monitoring/attentional support create new error classes.
- **[T]** Mode awareness is framed as a *continuous attentional* task, not a knowledge task — the operator must continuously track which mode + what it's doing. → *Licenses treating a mode indicator as a standing, always-present readout obligation, which is exactly vivarium's planned design.*
- **[T]** Dual causal attribution for mode errors: (a) operators' buggy mental models, AND (b) opaque, **scattered** interfaces — "numerous mode indications distributed over multiple displays" with delayed feedback loops. → *This is the highest-value transfer in this subsection: a 1995 finding naming "state is present but spread out and low-salience" as its own failure mechanism, independent of whether the content is correct.* Caveat: this phrasing is from a secondary summary's paraphrase, not verified against the primary.
- **[E]**, flagged unconfirmed by the extraction itself: aborted-takeoff scenario, 65% of pilots didn't realize automation controlled thrust, only 15% could accurately describe active mode afterward. Sample size, aircraft type, and whether these numbers are from this 1995 paper or an earlier 1992/1994 study it draws on could **not** be verified. **Do not cite this figure without finding the primary** — it's the single most quotable number in this subsection and the least verified.

### 2.6 Nikolic, Orr & Sarter (2004) — "Why Pilots Miss the Green Box"
*Source: tandfonline 10.1207/s15327108ijap1401_3 — **primary**, IJAP 14(1), 2004. **Three of five claims from this source were voted and all three SURVIVED (0R/3K each)** — this is the most solidly verified single source in the whole corpus.*

Verified (vote detail is in the corpus's own verification section, not reproduced in full here): abrupt onset/flashing does not reliably capture attention in data-rich operational displays; three separable display-context properties (color similarity, background motion, target eccentricity) each measurably degrade detection under a concurrent loaded task; and the failing real-world artifact is precisely a status chip — the green outline box drawn around the mode indication on the primary flight display, which pilots miss.

Not voted:
- **[E]**, flagged by the extraction as **not from the primary** — this figure comes from downstream attention-capture modeling literature's citation of Nikolic & Sarter's earlier work, not from reading this paper's own numbers: roughly an **8-percentage-point increase in miss rate per additional 10° of visual eccentricity**. → *Usable design number, but verify against the primary before quoting it with that precision.*
- **[T]** Authors' own methodological caution: lab attention findings don't transplant directly into complex interfaces — display context must be modeled as part of the signal. → *Directly applicable warning against importing Fitts's-law/contrast/salience lab results wholesale into a live simulation viewer with a dynamic, colored background.*

## 3. Ecological Interface Design (EID)

### 3.1 Vicente (2002) — EID review
*Source: sagepub 10.1518/0018720024494829 — **primary**, Human Factors 44(1), Spring 2002.*

- **[T+E]**, reviewed literature, not a single new study. EID's measured performance advantage over conventional design is **conditional on task regime** — holds specifically for problem-solving/unanticipated-event tasks, predicts little-to-no advantage for routine, well-anticipated operation. → *This is a genuinely falsifiable scoping claim, and it maps well onto vivarium's own framing: the instrument's job is catching physics violations nobody declared in advance, which is precisely the regime where EID's own literature claims an advantage.*
- **[E]**, dated fact: as of 2002 (~12 years after EID's founding), **no full-fidelity-simulator evaluation with professional workers had been run** — the evidence base was lab/microworld-scale, not field-validated, as of that date. Fixes EID's epistemic tier at "theoretically principled plus lab-empirical, not operationally validated," circa 2002.
- **[T]** EID's stated primary aim is supporting adaptation to novelty/change, not efficiency on known procedures — explicit design-goal statement, not a measured result.
- **[E]**, cross-domain but qualitative: applied at industry scale in ≥7 domains (process control, aviation, network management, software engineering, medicine, command & control, information retrieval), consistently surfacing previously-unidentified information requirements — a reproducible *analytic* payoff distinct from any rendering style.
- **[C]**, self-report from the framework's own originator: Vicente states significant unresolved challenges block confident industrial adoption as of 2002 — a caution against treating EID as a settled, turnkey method.

### 3.2 Vicente & Rasmussen (1992) — the founding EID paper
*Source: backend.orbit.dtu.dk (DTU Orbit) — **primary**, IEEE Trans. SMC 22(4), 1992.*

- **[T]** Formalizes fault detection as constraint-violation detection, and derives a hard content requirement from it: the interface must represent the **complete** set of goal-relevant constraints, because designers can't know in advance which will break. States explicitly that enumerating fault cases in advance "cannot, by definition, cope with unanticipated events." → *Direct theoretical grounding for "spot-check the physics without a pre-declared checklist," which is exactly what `vivarium explore` is for.*
- **[T]** EID's goal: don't force the operator's cognitive processing to a higher level (skill→rule→knowledge) than the task demands, while still supporting all three. Cites evidence people have "a definite preference for lower levels of cognitive control" and that knowledge-based behavior is "usually effortful and error prone." → *This is the mechanism behind the claim that a reflowing prose HUD is a defect: it forces analytic reading for state that should be skill/rule-level legible.*
- **[T]**, an important **self-disclaimer** worth flagging prominently: EID explicitly concedes that specifying the *right content* does not solve the *attention* problem — "making the necessary information available does not guarantee that it will be attended to or interpreted correctly." → *This directly corroborates vivarium's own attention-honesty framing (a true value only present in an unnoticed form isn't "said"), but it also means EID does not itself supply the fix — content-specification and attention-design are separate problems, and the corpus's alarm-management/regulatory sources are where the attention half actually gets addressed.*
- **[E]**, single study, tier (b): DURESS thermal-hydraulic simulation, expert/novice subjects, 25–30s viewing windows, no feedback/training/warning. Physical+Functional interface beat Physical-only on diagnosis, primarily for experts; diagnosis accuracy correlated with memory for *functional* variables, not physical ones. A "Random" control condition (simulated system violating physical law) ruled out a pure visual-form explanation. No effect sizes reported in this paper itself (deferred to a reference the harvest didn't fetch); authors label it "preliminary."
- **[T]**, authors' own scoping: generalizability beyond process control is explicitly an open question (aviation/library applications called "anecdotal"); the approach only works to the extent the designer already knows the governing constraints; robustness under sensor noise/model error is named as untested.

### 3.3 Borst, Flach & Ellerbroek (2015) — "Beyond Ecological Interface Design"
*Source: academia.edu-hosted preprint of Borst, Flach & Ellerbroek, IEEE THMS 45(2), 2015 (venue metadata in the harvest task itself was wrong — corrected in the source's own header). **primary**, though full IEEE text closed; recovered from publisher-derived abstract + two body paragraphs + figure captions.*

- **[T]**, and this is the sharpest tension in the whole corpus, worth stating plainly: this paper argues **the opposite of the prevailing minimalist-HUD instinct**. As automation increases, the correct move is to show *more* information ("opening the black box"), not less — the operator must monitor the machine's rationale, not just its output, for shared "common ground." → *This directly conflicts, in spirit, with Few's saliency-budget minimalism (§6.2), ISA-101's "gray is good" restraint (§4.5 below), and NUREG-0700's "minimize density for critical info" — none of these sources cite each other, and I don't think this is fully reconcilable by saying "they're talking about different things." It's a live disagreement between the EID tradition (more structure, more legible constraints) and the alarm-management/HMI-minimalism tradition (fewer things competing for attention) about where the actual lever is. I'd flag this as something worth deciding on purpose rather than inheriting by accident — vivarium's own instinct (move detail to an on-demand dump, keep the always-on surface sparse) sides with the minimalism camp, and that's a legitimate choice, but it's worth knowing it isn't the only defensible position in this literature.*
- **[T]**, refutes a specific fear directly: making constraints explicit makes sensor failures **more** detectable, not less — a bad reading becomes a visible violation of an explicit physical model rather than an unverifiable number. Names the failure condition explicitly: this benefit is contingent on mapping quality; unrepresented "deep structure" produces the exact ambiguities (including undetectable sensor failure) the approach is meant to prevent.
- **[T]**, dating/scoping fact: EID was introduced specifically for events *not anticipated* by the automation's designers, dated to Vicente & Rasmussen ~25 years prior (~1989–90), since spread to other safety-critical domains.
- **[T]**, diagnostic claim about current practice: modern glass cockpits are still substantially "single-sensor-single-indicator" (SSSI) — one display element per measured value, mostly lower-order information. Format modernization (analog dials → glass) did not fix this; the defect is architectural, not stylistic. → *Names, in avionics vocabulary, the exact structural failure of a variable-per-line debug readout — i.e., this is a precise description of what `vivarium explore`'s current chrome is doing wrong.*
- **[C]**, self-critical review from within the tradition (co-authored by an EID-adjacent insider, Flach): the authors report their own and others' explorations produced multiple usability concerns and misconceptions about EID's viability, and predict the "ecological" label will become redundant in favor of "productive thinking" as the organizing goal. The paper's own reference list documents a genuine internal dispute (a published critique of a key EID skill-acquisition study, plus the original authors' response). → *EID is contested from within its own tradition, not a settled consensus — worth not over-citing it as unified doctrine.*

## 4. Display clutter, density, and layout

### 4.1 2015 Human Factors clutter review
*Source: sagepub 10.1177/0018720814541145 — **primary**, Human Factors 57(1), 2015.*

- **[T]** Clutter is defined operationally — a display is only "cluttered," from an ergonomics standpoint, if it measurably degrades performance (specifically visual search). → *Directly refutes any appearance-only or aesthetic account of clutter; a dense panel with no measured search cost is, by this definition, not cluttered. This is a useful conceptual anchor even without a number attached.*
- **[T]**, explicit open-problem statement — see §1/Q1 above. No established quantitative density ceiling exists in the human-factors literature as of 2015.
- **[T]** Clutter measurement decomposes into four checkable families: image-processing algorithms, task-performance evaluation, subjective rating, eye tracking — a concrete evaluation menu, some of it runnable on a screenshot without human subjects.
- **[T]** No single metric is adequate on its own — the review's explicit recommendation is to combine complementary techniques, because clutter is multifaceted. → *A HUD evaluation resting on one measure (e.g. only a computed image score, or only "looks busy") is, by this review's own standard, methodologically insufficient.*
- **[T]** Frames clutter measurement as instrumental to attention management — "did the operator notice it" is the terminal criterion, not aesthetics.

### 4.2 Wickens & Carswell (1995) — Proximity Compatibility Principle
*Source: sagepub 10.1518/001872095779049408 — **primary**, Human Factors 37(3), Sept 1995. Full text paywalled — abstract-level only.*

- **[T]** PCP is a *conditional* layout rule, not a general "group things nicely" heuristic: elements that must be combined in one mental operation ("task proximity") should be rendered close in perceptual space ("display proximity"). Falsifiable and directly testable for a status-chip strip — predicts a measurable cost when readouts that must be read jointly (e.g. a mode indicator and the freshness state that qualifies it) are perceptually separated.
- **[T]** PCP is explicitly *not* monotonic/single-mechanism: the paper distinguishes multiple forms of task proximity and multiple design manipulations of display proximity (spatial nearness vs. object integration vs. color/coding linkage), each running through a different information-processing mechanism. → *"Put related things together" underspecifies the prescription; which manipulation you use matters.*
- **[T+E]** Empirical warrant is a review across four domains (aviation, graph design, display layout, decision aiding), not one new experiment — tier (a) theoretical statement + literature synthesis, with tier (b) support residing in the reviewed primary studies (none of which the harvest fetched, so no effect size is recoverable here).
- **[C→T]** Positions PCP as design-prescriptive for safety-critical panels specifically, aviation named as a review context. **Cross-reference to §7.6**: Caroux & Isbister (2016) directly *tested* PCP-conforming layouts against unmodified commercial game HUDs and found familiarity beat PCP-conformance for the originals, though PCP won among the *modified* layouts only. That's a genuine partial empirical test of this exact 1995 theoretical claim, in a domain PCP wasn't designed for — worth reading together.

### 4.3 Rosenholtz et al. (2007) — measures of visual clutter
*Source: jov.arvojournals.org — **primary**, Journal of Vision, 2007.*

- **[T+E]** Visual clutter is image-computable: three algorithmic measures (Feature Congestion, Subband Entropy, Edge Density) take an arbitrary raster image and return a clutter score with no knowledge of display semantics. → *Falsifiable and directly runnable: score two candidate HUD layouts from screenshots and check they're distinguishable.*
- **[E]**, magnitude not recoverable (full text paywalled): the measures correlate with visual search performance well enough to substitute for "set size" in Guided-Search-class models — the closest published account of a density-vs-noticing tradeoff that generalizes beyond countable-element displays.
- **[T]** Feature Congestion is *defined* as exactly the alerting problem: how hard it would be to add a new item that reliably draws attention. → *Formal restatement of the dark-cockpit intuition — quiet backgrounds buy alarm salience. This is the single most directly-applicable finding in this subsection for scoring whether your freshness alarm will actually be noticed against a busy globe render.*
- **[E]** Color variability is a genuine, separable clutter contributor — captured by Feature Congestion but *not* by Edge Density or Subband Entropy. → *A multi-hued status strip's attentional cost will be under-reported by edge-count or compressibility-style metrics; color variance needs its own accounting.*
- **[T]** The harm mechanism is named explicitly (crowding, masking, occlusion-driven recognition loss, harder segmentation, slower search) — quantity is not the only driver; disorganization/reflowing is a distinct cost from item count, though these three measures don't themselves capture positional *stability over time*.

### 4.4 NUREG-0700 Rev. 3 (2020-07) — density and layout guidance
*Source: nrc.gov — **primary**, NRC staff report. See §5.3 for the validity-taxonomy claims from this same source.*

- **[C]** Guideline 1.5-8: caps packing density at 50% overall / 25% for alphanumeric-heavy displays, and instructs *minimizing* (not just capping) density for critical information. Provenance code traces to Smith & Mosier 1986 (NASA/MITRE handbook) — codified handbook practice, not an experimentally derived threshold. Covered in §1/Q1 above.

## 5. Uncertainty and staleness visualization

### 5.1 Padilla, Kay & Hullman (2022) — uncertainty visualization
*Source: space.ucmerced.edu — **primary**, book chapter, 2022.*

- **[E]**, replicated finding (Joslyn & LeClerc, cited within): viewers substitute an uncertainty display for a deterministic reading — mistaking 95% CI error-bar caps for high/low temperature bounds — and the error **survives an on-screen key and explicit instruction**. Critically, the same error was **not** found for textual descriptions of the same information. → *Gloss check: this is a partial refutation of "a caveat rendered only as text isn't a caveat" — text is the condition where the misreading did NOT occur here; the graphic is what manufactured it. The design lesson isn't "draw it instead of writing it," it's "a graphic can install false certainty that a legend cannot remove." Worth being precise about this when citing it — it cuts the opposite direction from what you might assume walking in.*
- **[E]**, replicated across multiple domains (map contours, Google Maps blue dot, bar charts): any hard visual boundary makes viewers treat inside/outside as categorically different populations, subconsciously and instruction-resistant even when they can correctly state the right interpretation afterward. Already flagged in §1/Q2 as a risk for whatever staleness-threshold color-flip you pick.
- **[E]** Hypothetical Outcome Plots (animated random draws, ~400–500ms/frame) empirically outperform static error bars, icon arrays, line ensembles, and violin plots for lay viewers on uncertainty comprehension. **But** the chapter itself names the exact failure mode your no-depiction-without-referent norm exists to prevent: viewers may read the animation's frame sequence as an event unfolding in time. → *This is simultaneously the strongest empirical case *for* generated/non-referent imagery in the whole corpus, and the source of the caveat that would convict it under your own norm. Worth citing both halves together if this ever comes up — HOPs are a genuine dead end for vivarium specifically, for a reason the corpus itself supplies.*
- **[E]** Legibility-degradation encoding (blur/gray-desaturation proportional to uncertainty) — covered in §1/Q2. Cost: degrades point-lookup performance, a real trade-off to name if you consider it.
- **[T+E]** Displaying uncertainty at all is not guaranteed to help — no one-size-fits-all technique exists. Concrete counter-intuitive instance: in ensemble hurricane displays, viewers *overreact* to a single ensemble member striking their location and don't correspondingly react to one narrowly missing — and the overreaction is **stronger with fewer members shown** (1-of-9 worse than 1-of-33), only partially fixed by training. → *A caution against "sparse representative sample" as a decluttering strategy for probabilistic displays specifically.*

### 5.2 Means-ends ATC study (2016, IFAC-PapersOnLine)
*Source: sciencedirect S2405896316321504 — **primary**, IFAC symposium paper, 2016. Small, non-controller sample: N=16 (8/group), TU Delft students/researchers, not certified ATCOs; fixed 7.5nm ADS-B offset fault, scripted advisories.*

- **[E]**, small N, non-professional population: making the means-ends link explicit (hover on an aircraft highlights its derived conflict-zone) significantly improved fault localization (WHICH sensor failed) in both low- and high-complexity scenarios (χ²(2)=4.923, p=.027; χ²(2)=5.208, p=.022).
- **[E]** Noticing something's wrong and identifying what's wrong are **separable capabilities with different display requirements**: both interface conditions let operators sense an anomaly, only the means-ends-linked group could localize it. → *An unreliability signal that only says "something is off" is a strictly weaker deliverable than one carrying its own referent — directly relevant if your freshness alarm design is tempted to be a generic warning icon rather than something that points at what's stale.*
- **[E]** Density measurably degrades fault detection with a **named mechanism** (not just "clutter is bad"): unstructured/high-complexity scenarios produced significantly lower detection within-subjects (χ²(1)=4.500, p=.034), attributed to overlapping derived glyphs occluding the inconsistency.
- **[E]** The sensor failure was made perceptible by **geometric disagreement between two co-displayed representations of the same referent** (derived Solution Space Diagram vs. raw radar plan-view) — not by an alarm or text caveat. → *This is a concrete instance of an EID-flavored redundancy/cross-check mechanism (§3), distinct from an annunciator-style mechanism. Note the distinction from §1/Q2: this mechanism catches a sensor lying about a *value*; it wouldn't catch a cache that's simply old but internally consistent — same limitation as EID generally.*
- **[E]**, mixed reliability: the linked-interface group was slower to respond to advisories, but the paper's own reported statistic here (F(1,14)=206.249, p=.051) is **internally implausible** — that F magnitude cannot yield p≈.05 at those degrees of freedom — so the harvest itself flags this specific number as unconfirmed/likely-erroneous in the source; treat the response-time cost as unconfirmed. The trust-reduction/order-effect finding (experiencing one sensor failure depressed trust even in subsequent failure-free scenarios) rests on a separate, plausible statistic (F(1,14)=19.283, p=.001) and is more trustworthy.

### 5.3 NUREG-0700 Rev. 3 — validity/freeze/mode guidance
*Source: nrc.gov — **primary**. (Density claim already covered in §4.4.)*

- **[C, regulatory-adjacent]** Guideline 1.1-23: freeze mode requires an "obvious reminder," explicitly recommending a flashing message. 2.5.4-6 requires frozen displays be labeled as frozen. 2.5.4-7 goes further: the operator must be advised when a significant *underlying* change has occurred that the frozen display isn't showing. → *This is precisely the vivarium stale-cache case, stated by a regulator as mandatory and self-announcing, not optional.*
- **[C, regulatory-adjacent]** Guideline 14.2-2: sensor/instrument failures must produce "distinct display changes" directly indicating invalidity (e.g. offscale indication). §14.3 imposes a **four-state validity taxonomy** that must be shown on the display face: 14.3-3 (invalid must be indicated), 14.3-4 (**unvalidated** — checks couldn't be run — must be its own distinct state, not collapsed into "invalid"), 14.3-5 (quality indicator with enumerated values: valid / invalid / unvalidated / numerical estimate). → *The "unvalidated" state is the notable one — the standard refuses to conflate "known bad" with "not checkable," and requires placeholder-vs-measurement to be visible "so operators can exercise judgment in interpreting them." This maps almost exactly onto a placeholder-value chip state for vivarium.*
- **[C, regulatory-adjacent]** Guideline 9.4-1/9.4-5/2.1-34/2.7.6-9: mode indication must be always-present and highly salient, with notice both before and at an automatic mode change, and "the user should not have to query the system to determine the current mode." → *Direct ratification of a persistent mode chip over a mode-line buried in scrolling text.*
- **[T]** Designers must affirmatively analyze whether an instrument's *own degradation* could be mistaken by the operator for a real process disturbance (naming human reliability analysis, confusion matrices, misdiagnosis tree analysis as methods; grounded in IEEE Std. 497-2002 §6.5 and IEEE Std. 603-1998). → *Independent regulator-side statement of vivarium's own norm that a view must not manufacture appearances a trained eye will read as physics — here framed from the failure direction: a broken sensor making the plant *look* like it's transitioning.*

## 6. Dashboard design genre

### 6.1 TVCG 2019 dashboard-design-space taxonomy
*Source: dl.acm.org / IEEE TVCG 25(1), 2019 (VIS/InfoVis 2018) — **primary**, ACM DL 403'd, recovered from authors' preprint.*

- **[C, with a measured coding-reliability statistic]**: descriptive taxonomy from qualitative open coding of 83 dashboards, 15 factors → 7 genres, Cohen's κ=0.64 (86.5% agreement). Authors explicitly state they consulted no users or designers and the framework "may fail to isolate best practices." → *Epistemic tier: craft-consensus systematization with one measured methodology statistic — not evidence that any of the 15 factors improves noticing, accuracy, or speed. Grade this carefully if it gets cited; it's the field's own taxonomy, not a performance study.*
- **[T]** Dashboard design principles are **not genre-portable**: layout rules tuned for glance-ability in operational dashboards may fail for communication/narrative dashboards. Stated operational-genre rule: order views by **criticality**, not by temporal/semantic relationship, even when that breaks the data's natural order. → *Directly licenses a fixed-position criticality-ranked chip strip, and simultaneously warns that guidance mined from narrative/communication dashboard literature doesn't transfer to vivarium's instrument-panel use case.*
- **[C]** "Alerting+Notification" and "Benchmarks" coded as distinct data-semantics dimensions, distributed non-uniformly by genre: >3/4 of decision-making-cluster dashboards carried benchmarks (gauges, threshold marks, status lights), vs. neither in the public-communication cluster. Alerting is defined narrowly — live data connection + user/model-defined thresholds raising an explicit alert — separating "a number is displayed" from "the display announces the number is out of bounds."
- **[T]**, weak citation chain — covered in §1/Q1 above (single 1979 accounting-systems study is the sole backing for the density-vs-uncertainty tradeoff claim).
- **[T]**, qualitative case-study/policy-literature tier, not controlled experiment — covered in §1/Q2 above (dashboard form manufactures unearned credibility). No measured design that *succeeds* at conveying uncertainty is reported by this paper.

### 6.2 Stephen Few — Information Dashboard Design
*Source: **secondary** — the assigned Amazon listing returned no book content; quotes are from Few's companion white paper "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (Perceptual Edge, Feb 2006), which the paper states is based on a book chapter, with the 2nd-ed. book (Analytics Press, Aug 2013) confirmed by an independent review to retain the same definitions. This entire subsection is **[C]** — craft consensus, explicitly not measured; Few's own evidentiary standard for most of these claims is his personal introspection on a single screenshot ("if you're like me…").*

- **[C]** Single-screen/no-scroll is *definitional* for Few, not stylistic — mechanism claimed is short-term-memory capacity ("a few chunks"). This is the craft-consensus ancestor of the vivarium chip-strip intuition.
- **[C]** Uniform visual prominence = zero attention direction = outright display failure, by Few's own framing. Pairs with a three-stage monitoring model (overview → notice what needs attention → drill for detail) — the same shape as vivarium's intended repair (always-present chips for stages 1–2, on-demand capture dump for stage 3).
- **[C]** A bare current value, with no comparative context (target/history) and no separate fast qualitative cue (good/satisfactory/bad), is a design defect. → *Cross-reference §7.5: this pairs unusually well with the yorku 2018 study's measured finding that numeric readouts beat bars/icons for precision tasks — combined, the two suggest "number for precision + one separate qualitative color cue for 'is this OK,'" not a bar or gauge replacing the number. Worth noting that pairing is my synthesis, not stated by either source; Few and the yorku study don't cite each other.* Few also forbids precision beyond task need (e.g. "$3.8M" over the full decimal figure).
- **[C]**, one uncited figure: ~10% of males / ~1% of females are color-blind (roughly consistent with standard clinical figures for European-descent populations, but Few cites no source). Color's highlighting power is asserted to deplete with overuse — reserve it for the exception channel.
- **[C]** Explicitly condemns skeuomorphic instrument styling (gauges, meters, traffic lights, LED-look readouts) as pure decoration the viewer must process before reaching data, reporting a novelty-decay failure mode ("day-one ooh-and-aah, abandoned by end of week"). → *A direct, if unmeasured, warning against dressing the panel in cockpit clothing — matches the borrow-the-discipline-not-the-vocabulary framing in your brief almost exactly.*

## 7. Game HUD taxonomy and attention empirics

### 7.1 Bowman, Elmqvist & Jankun-Kelly (2012) — "Toward Visualization for Games"
*Source: cs.au.dk (authors' accepted preprint) — **primary**, IEEE TVCG 18(11), 2012. The canonical academic framework for the domain.*

- **[C]**, load-bearing methodological finding: **contains no empirical evaluation whatsoever** — no user study, no participants, no eye-tracking (verified by the extraction via term search: zero hits). Sole stated validation is expressive coverage (classifies five recurring design patterns). → *Every design assertion in this paper sits at craft-consensus tier, despite being the field's most-cited reference work.*
- **[C]** The bars-over-numbers HUD convention (health bar vs. percentage) is documented here as designer *belief*: attributed explicitly to what "the game designers feel," no citation to preattentive-processing literature, no measurement. → *Game HUD lore treats this as established; the field's own foundational survey records it as unmeasured belief.*
- **[C]**, genre-conditional, no data adjudicating between two competing speculative explanations offered: the "games are removing the HUD" trend holds for FPS/RPG (diegetic-immersion grounds) but not RTS, which retains prominent HUDs. → *For an overhead/globe instrument specifically, the "remove the HUD" craft lore is evidence from the wrong genre — RTS-adjacent framing (bird's-eye, abstract markers, information readouts) is the closer analogy.*
- **[T]**, the taxonomy's own structure: Temporal Usage dimension already separates always-visible continuous status from call-up "intermittent" display, justified on distraction grounds; names flight-simulator heads-down views as the exemplar intermittent class. → *The canonical taxonomy independently arrives at the always-on-status-vs-on-demand-dump split vivarium is proposing.*
- **[C]** **Named dead end**: the five-dimension design space has *no axis* for urgency, alerting, criticality, or data reliability — verified by full-text term search (zero hits for alarm/alert/notification/uncertainty/stale/reliability/salience/clutter/density). → *The 2012 academic game-visualization literature offers nothing on alarm prioritization or unreliability announcement; those questions genuinely have to come from the safety-critical disciplines, confirming your brief's own scope-decomposition premise.*

### 7.2 ACM in-game UI taxonomy (2024)
*Source: dl.acm.org 10.1145/3681716.3689454 — **primary**, CHI PLAY 2024 short paper, ACM full text inaccessible (Cloudflare-blocked on every retrieval path tried, including Wayback and the Chrome extension); everything below is metadata-derived (abstract + full 49-item reference list via Crossref), not body text.*

- **[C]**, self-declared: the taxonomy is explicitly "an initial version," 5-page short paper. Craft/conceptual proposal tier, not empirically established.
- **[T]**, metadata-derived: the paper's scope decomposes in-game UI visualization into system-state / environment / own-character-state — a three-class partition that converges with the safety-critical framing even though the literatures don't cite each other.
- **[C]**, verified by resolving all 49 references: theoretical scaffolding is entirely InfoVis design-space lineage (Shneiderman, Card & Mackinlay, Chi, Tory & Möller, Brehmer & Munzner, etc.) plus game-analytics visualization — **not** an HCI/attention lineage. Evidence base is a convenience corpus of ~25 named commercial games sourced via a practitioner screenshot archive, affinity-mapped on a Miro board — no sampling frame, no coder-agreement statistic, no player measurement.
- **[C]**, a checkable **negative** finding worth taking seriously: **none of the 49 references** are Fagerholt & Lorentzon's "Beyond the HUD" taxonomy, any games-user-research or heuristic source (Pinelle, Desurvire, Hodent), or any human-factors/avionics/alarm-management/EID source. → *Confirms, empirically (via bibliography resolution, not assertion), your brief's own premise that these are three mutually non-citing literatures as of late 2024 — a genuine structural finding, not a dead end. Worth citing this specific negative result if the segment wants to justify why it's synthesizing across literatures nobody else has bridged.*

### 7.3 Kriglstein, Wallner & Pohl (CHI 2014) — gameplay-visualization comparison
*Source: dl.acm.org 10.1145/2556288.2557317 — **primary**, CHI '14, ACM DL 403'd, abstract-level recovery.*

- **[E]**, N=29 players, 3 representations (heatmap + 2 clustering-based). → *A controlled comparative user study exists for game telemetry visualization — this is not purely craft assertion, though effect sizes sit behind the paywall.*
- **[E]** Encoding choice is task-dependent, not globally rankable: heatmaps for hot-spot detection, cluster representations for comparing variables. → *Directly bears on the design of vivarium's on-demand capture dump: "where is the anomaly" and "is variable A consistent with variable B" are different tasks needing different idioms.*
- **[E]** Relationship discovery between variables was attributed specifically to the cluster representations, not the heatmap — a measured limitation of the dominant game-telemetry idiom (heatmaps) as an analytic instrument.
- **[E]**, population caveat: participants were players interpreting gameplay data, not trained analysts — closer to "domain-competent operator spot-checking a simulation" than to "developer using an analytics dashboard," which bounds the transfer claim in both directions.

### 7.4 Caroux (2022) — four-experiment physical/semantic HUD study
*Source: tandfonline 10.1080/0144929X.2022.2081609 — **primary**, published issue Behaviour & IT 2023, 42(10). Publisher page and HAL both blocked; recovered via Wayback snapshot of the authors' accepted manuscript.*

- **[E]**, N=40 (The Witcher III, HUD area 9% vs 15% of screen) and N=36 (CS:GO, red vs. blue HUD color): near-zero effects on performance and subjective experience for both manipulations (η²p < .01 in most tests). → *One of the strongest, most directly-actionable null results in the corpus: physical HUD size and color, on their own, measurably don't matter much.*
- **[E]**, League of Legends, N not stated in the extraction beyond the F-test df (76): removing task-**relevant** ("main") elements (avatar status, mini-map orientation) caused large, highly significant decrements (η²p=.41 and .67); removing task-**irrelevant** ("secondary") elements (score, pseudonyms, item counts) left performance near full-HUD levels. → *It's task-value, not amount of chrome, that governs measured outcomes — the single cleanest empirical statement of the "criticality, not count" principle in the whole corpus.*
- **[E]**, Zelda: Skyward Sword, N=41: removing control-mapping HUD elements changed neither progress (F(1,39)=1.11, p=.30) nor immersion (F(1,39)=2.41, p=.13) — a direct **non-replication** of the craft belief (and a specific earlier study, Iacovides et al. 2015) that minimal HUD improves expert immersion.
- **[E]** Expertise moderates sensitivity to high-value readouts: experienced-player advantage was present with full HUD and with secondary elements removed, but vanished entirely when *main* elements were removed (interaction p<.001) — removing a task-critical readout erases the skilled operator's advantage disproportionately.
- **[E]** Perceptibility ≠ behavioral effect: 78% of experienced players consciously noticed the red/blue color change (vs. 6% of less-experienced), yet no performance or affect measure moved. Correspondingly, the salience/effort arm of Wickens et al.'s SEEV attention model **failed in 3 of 4 experiments** in this paper, while its value/expectancy arm held. → *A well-powered, direct empirical strike against "make it more salient/bigger/differently colored" as a fix, when the underlying value/relevance of the element hasn't changed.*

### 7.5 HUD taxonomy empirical test (2018)
*Source: yorku.ca/mack — **primary**, 2018. Four within-subjects experiments, expert FPS players, custom Unity game, ammunition/health/weapon/navigation tasks.*

- **[E]** Headline result: display *type* (HUD / diegetic / spatial) is not the determining variable — no class won universally; performance was determined by properties of the specific display (precision, richness, position relative to task). → *A direct empirical refutation of the common craft claim that diegetic displays are inherently superior — and a named contradiction of the prevailing "Beyond the HUD" recommendation literature, stated by the authors themselves.*
- **[E]** For a precision task (health, judging whether to disengage): **numeric readouts beat bar and icon encodings by a large margin** — ~76% successful-escape rate for number-on-HUD-top vs. ~46% for bar-on-HUD-top; icon conditions diverged sharply on the health level at which players escaped. Attributed to precision: bars/icons don't afford reading a percentage range. → *Directly bears on your chip design: for anything where precise value matters, a number beats a bar or gauge. See §6.2 cross-reference — this pairs with Few's "comparative context + qualitative cue" prescription.*
- **[E]** For a spatial task (waypoint navigation): an in-world spatial display (navigation line) massively outperformed an abstract compass panel (~21.6s vs. ~56.4s to reach waypoint; ~1.03 vs. ~2.43 step-errors; ~2% vs. ~42% incomplete trials); a rotating mini-map was competitive with the navigation line. → *Generalizable design rule: encode spatial information spatially and co-located with its referent; encode scalar state numerically. Match encoding to information type, not to a house HUD style.*
- **[E]** Subjective helpfulness ratings tracked objective performance fairly consistently — evidence *against* the standard human-factors caution that preference diverges from performance, at least for expert users on well-defined instrument-reading tasks (though at least one condition still had ~45% of participants report a display as actively hindering them).

### 7.6 Diegesis and Immersion study (CHI PLAY 2015)
*Source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk — **primary**, CHI PLAY 2015.*

- **[E]**, Study 2, N=24 (10 experts, 14 novices), between-subjects, cells N=4–8: removing the entire non-diegetic HUD (Battlefield 3) raised immersion (IEQ) for **experts only**; novices essentially unchanged. Neither main effect was significant on its own; the effect was entirely carried by the interaction (F(1,20)=4.32, p=.051, partial η²=.178), with an expert simple effect at p=.058. → *Gloss check: the checkable statement is expertise-conditional, not "less HUD is better" as often quoted — and it rests on p-values just above the conventional .05 threshold, interpreted via an effect-size/severity argument rather than classical significance.*
- **[E]** The measured cost is specifically an **attention and control** cost, not a difficulty or affective one — the two largest and only conventionally-significant interactions were on Cognitive Involvement (effort/focus items, p<.05, η²p=.28) and Control (ease of interface use, p<.01, η²p=.33). Emotional Involvement, Real-World Dissociation, and Challenge showed no condition effect. → *Direct empirical support for "displayed-but-unneeded information is an attention tax," measurable independently of task difficulty.*
- **[T]**, authors' own post-hoc mechanism, **not** measured (no gaze data collected): experts attend to displayed elements *because* they're displayed, presuming relevance, even when expertise makes the information redundant — an element can be a necessary scaffold early and a net cost later. → *Predicts that a readout an expert no longer needs still costs attention until removed; makes "what to display" a function of operator-expertise-state, not a fixed panel property.*
- **[E]**, Study 1, N=9, within-subjects, counterweight worth flagging: removing the HUD didn't block engagement/enjoyment (both n.s.), **but** the Core Elements of Gaming Experience scale trended *toward keeping* the HUD with a medium-large effect (t=-1.97, p=.08, d=.66) — the same paper reports a non-significant trend pointing the opposite direction at the engagement/facilitation level from what it finds at the immersion level. → *The same authors' own data shows "remove the HUD" isn't a clean win even within this single paper — worth citing both halves together.*
- **[C]**, as of 2015: the game-industry doctrine that diegetic integration increases immersion (Dead Space, "Beyond the HUD," Stonehouse's Gamasutra piece) had **no empirical backing** — this paper doesn't supply it either, because it manipulated *removal*, never diegetic re-presentation of the same information. Authors themselves admit their diegetic/non-diegetic taxonomy is unstable (a scoped weapon's crosshair, or a walkover notification, arguably belongs to both categories). → *Any claim of the form "diegetic presentation is more immersive than non-diegetic presentation of the same information" remains genuinely untested, by this paper's own admission.*

### 7.7 Caroux & Isbister (2016) — eye-tracking HUD study
*Source: hal.science hal-01455547 — **primary**, IJHCS 87, 2016. **Access note the harvest agent itself flagged**: hal.science requires an Anubis proof-of-work challenge that blocks automated fetches; the harvest recovered the full 56-page preprint via a 2018 Wayback snapshot and independently re-verified quotes by grep against a freshly re-downloaded copy — unusually thorough provenance for this corpus. The harvest agent also flagged a filename-collision hazard with a sibling agent's scratch files mid-task, caught only because two reads disagreed.*

- **[E]**, small (n=15, 3–5/cell), authors flag as needing dedicated study: transient/non-permanent HUD elements (messages, warnings — conventionally placed center-screen) drew very few eye fixations compared to permanently-displayed elements, Experiment 1 eye-tracking across two commercial games (FPS, RTS). → *Covered in §1/Q2. Measured, independent corroboration of the FAA's "text change alone is not a valid attention cue" rule (§2.2), from a completely different domain and method.*
- **[E]**, N=48, rated modified screenshots, both genres: the unmodified full HUD was rated **highest**, HUDs with permanent elements removed rated **lowest**, empty "control" HUD worst. → *Measured counter-evidence to the game-industry craft position (the paper cites designer Wilson 2006) that HUDs damage immersion — though note this is rated *preference* on static screenshots, not performance or measured immersion in live play.*
- **[E]** Familiarity/convention beat the ergonomic principle in rated preference: HUDs re-laid-out to conform to PCP (§4.2) were rated **lower** than unmodified commercial originals for both genres — the authors' own hypothesis was refuted. PCP only surfaced as a factor once the *original* was off the table: among modified layouts only, the PCP-conforming arrangement won and the anti-PCP arrangement lost. Paper's own explanation for the originals' win: both put most elements in a single line at the bottom of the screen (became the paper's Recommendation 3). → *Directly cross-references §4.2 — a genuine partial empirical test of a 1995 theoretical claim, with a mixed result: PCP holds once familiarity/convention is controlled for, but doesn't override strong existing convention.*
- **[E]**, methodologically important: player self-report about HUD use is unreliable and can be flatly contradicted by gaze data — novice players stated in interviews they didn't use the HUD, but eye-tracking showed they fixated it with no significant difference from experts; conversely, subjective interview data showed clear expert/novice differences that gaze data barely showed. → *Implication for any future vivarium instrument evaluation: asking an operator "did you look at that readout" is not evidence they did or didn't.*
- **[E]**, applies Wickens et al.'s SEEV model, one confounded observation: RTS HUD occupied 27.75% of screen vs. 8.08% for FPS, and received significantly more fixations (F(1,11)=14.62, p<.01, η²p=.57) — attributed to SEEV's salience factor, but screen-area share and information-usefulness varied together in this design, so the two SEEV factors aren't separated here.

## 9. Discrepancy flag — the single most load-bearing number in the corpus is internally inconsistent

Two secondary sources, both citing EEMUA 191 for the same quantity ("how many alarms per hour can an operator manage"), give **different numbers**: chemengonline.com says ~12/hr long-term average with an 18/hr action limit; humanfactors101.com says ≤1 alarm per 10 minutes (=6/hr) for steady state. Neither source is the primary standard (both are paywalled/purchasable, not in this corpus), so this can't be resolved from what's here.

A plausible reconciliation — **stated as my own recollection from training, not verified in this session, and explicitly not to be treated as sourced**: EEMUA 191 is commonly described as having multiple acceptability bands rather than one ceiling (something like "very likely acceptable" below ~1/10min, "likely acceptable/manageable" up to ~2/10min, "likely unacceptable" above that) — which would make both numbers technically real, just naming different bands. I have not verified this against any source in this corpus or via a fresh fetch, so treat it as a hypothesis to check, not a resolution. Given this is the number your Q1 answer would most want to cite precisely, and it's currently unreconciled, I'd treat it as the single highest-priority item to verify before it goes in a segment with a specific number attached.

## 11. What surprised me / what I'd weight highest

1. **The EID-vs-minimalism tension (§3.3) is real and unresolved, not a false conflict.** Borst/Flach/Ellerbroek's "show more, not less, as automation increases" sits in direct tension with Few's saliency-budget minimalism, ISA-101's "gray is good," and NUREG's own "minimize density for critical info" — and none of these sources cite each other. I don't think vivarium's instinct (sparse always-on surface, detail on demand) is *wrong*, but it's worth knowing it's a chosen side in a live disagreement between two safety-critical design traditions, not the uncontested position.

2. **The three-literature convergence on "misleading beats absent, and worse" (§1/Q2, §2.2, §5.3, §6.1)** is the strongest single finding in the corpus — nuclear, aviation, and visualization research arrived at the same principle independently, with none of them citing each other. That's the kind of convergence worth actually building the segment's core claim around.

3. **Game-HUD craft lore takes real, measured hits from within its own literature.** Caroux 2022's near-zero effects for HUD size/color, its non-replication of "minimal HUD helps immersion," and its finding that noticing (78%) doesn't imply behavior change; the yorku 2018 study's flat refutation of "diegetic beats non-diegetic universally"; Caroux & Isbister's finding that PCP loses to familiarity for real commercial HUDs — these are not craft-consensus-vs-nothing, they're measured studies directly contradicting widely-repeated design lore, inside the game literature itself. If vivarium was inclined to trust "conventional wisdom" from game HUD design at all, this corpus gives specific, cited reasons not to for several of the most commonly repeated claims (diegetic superiority, minimal-HUD-helps-immersion, salience-fixes-matter).

4. **The EEMUA number discrepancy (§9)** is the most consequential unresolved item — it's precisely the number Q1 wants, from precisely the standard everyone points to, and two respectable secondary sources disagree by a factor of 2. Worth one real verification pass if this figure is going to be cited with precision.

5. **The corpus's own "quote genuine, gloss overreaches" pattern (§2.3) turned out to be extremely consistent within a single source** — 3 for 3 on 14 CFR 25.1322. That's strong enough evidence to treat as a property of *that specific extraction*, not a general indictment of the harvest — the sibling sources that went to vote elsewhere (Nikolic 2004: 3 for 3 **survived**) show the harvest wasn't uniformly over-glossing everything, just this one regulation.
